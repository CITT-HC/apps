A simple redesign pattern

Keep the important learning goal. Change the evidence you collect about how the student learned, decided, checked, revised, and can explain the work. You do not need every element below on every assignment.

Independent attempt or prediction
Allowed resources or AI support
Visible revision or reasoning
Brief verification

Design principle: collect enough evidence to make the learning claim defensible, not so much process evidence that students experience every assignment as surveillance.

Step 1

Describe the course and assignment

Course format changes what forms of process evidence and verification are practical. Assignment type changes which evidence is most useful.

A short description is enough. You will build the redesigned version below.

Step 2

Protect the learning claim

Before deciding whether AI is allowed, identify what the grade is supposed to tell you about the student. This is an assessment-validity question, not primarily a technology question.

Ask: “What should a successful student be able to do because of this assignment—and which parts must they be able to do without the AI doing that thinking for them?”

Research grounding: Assessment Validity in AI Education .

Which capabilities does this assignment assess?

Prefer an observable performance: analyze, calculate, create, defend, diagnose, evaluate, interpret, justify, revise, synthesize, or apply.

Step 3

Set a task-specific AI-use boundary

A useful policy tells students what is permitted on this particular task, why the boundary exists, what must remain their own work, and what they should disclose.

Framing matters: explain the learning reason for the boundary. “Do not use AI” is clearer when students also know what capability they are practicing and why they need to build it themselves.

Step 4

Choose redesign strategies

Recommended strategies are highlighted after you choose an assignment type. Use only the strategies that produce meaningful evidence for your learning goal.

More is not automatically better. A proposal, three logs, two videos, screenshots, full AI transcripts, an oral defense, and a final reflection may make an assignment burdensome without improving validity. Choose the smallest useful evidence set.

Step 5

Browse redesign examples

Examples change when you change the assignment type. Use them as patterns, not templates that must be copied in full.

Step 6

Choose process evidence and verification

Verification should answer a learning question—not try to infer authorship from writing style. Keep it short and proportionate.

Useful process evidence

Video is a verification option, not a universal requirement. When speaking on camera is not part of the construct, an equivalent written, audio, annotated, asynchronous, or live option may produce the same evidence with fewer barriers.

Suggested grading emphasis

Make at least some of the meaningful process visible in the grade, rather than grading only the polished final product.

Step 7

Run an equity, accessibility, and proportionality check

An assessment can become harder to outsource while also becoming harder for students with disabilities, caregiving obligations, unreliable technology, limited private space, or language barriers. Protect the learning construct—not unnecessary barriers.

Check each item you have addressed

Do not create a “cheater profile.” Integrity problems are not explained by one personality, culture, language background, disability status, grade level, or demographic group. Design the environment, expectations, support, evidence, and follow-up instead.

Step 8

Build the redesigned assignment

The worksheet will combine your selections into a student-facing assignment description with the learning purpose, sequence, AI-use policy, evidence requirements, verification, grading, and accessibility language.

Why redesign this way?

Academic integrity in the AI era is not simply a contest between better generation and better detection. The stronger educational question is: what evidence lets us make a defensible claim about what this student knows, can do, and can explain?

Misconception: “Any AI use is cheating.”

AI use is not one behavior. Brainstorming, proofreading, generating a complete answer, critiquing a model, and receiving a hint can have very different relationships to the learning goal.

A better rule is to decide where AI would substitute for the capability being assessed, where it can safely support it, and where using AI is itself part of the intended competency.

Knowledge-base synthesis: Academic Integrity; Assessment Validity in AI Education.

Misconception: “A finished product proves learning.”

Assisted performance and independent learning are not the same thing. Generative AI can produce a plausible product while bypassing parts of the reasoning, retrieval, drafting, calculation, or revision that the assignment was supposed to exercise.

Collect a small amount of process evidence and, when important, a short transfer or explanation task.

Knowledge-base synthesis: Cognitive Offloading; Assessment Validity in AI Education.

“Cognitive surrender” is really an over-reliance problem

“Cognitive surrender” is useful plain language, but the research construct is better described as cognitive offloading and, at the harmful end, over-reliance.

Offloading is not inherently bad. Calculators, notes, search, and AI can free attention for higher-level work. The risk appears when the tool substitutes for the very thinking or practice the learner needs to develop.

A practical safeguard is: think first → use AI second → reflect and verify afterward.

Knowledge-base synthesis: Cognitive Offloading.

Misconception: “If AI sounds confident, it is probably right.”

Fluency is not evidence. Productive AI literacy includes trust calibration: matching confidence to the evidence, task, stakes, and reliability of the system rather than to confident wording.

Teach the habit: AI proposes; the learner verifies. Students should check consequential claims against appropriate sources, tests, calculations, evidence, or disciplinary criteria.

Knowledge-base synthesis: Trust Calibration; Student Misconceptions about AI.

Misconception: “AI detectors prove who wrote the work.”

Detection is task-dependent and can produce both false positives and false negatives. A score does not establish how a submission was produced. Detector outputs also create fairness and automation-bias concerns.

If concern arises, use the assignment rule, process evidence, source checks, substantive inconsistencies, student explanation, and ordinary due process. A detector score should not be treated as guilt probability.

Knowledge-base synthesis: Academic Integrity.

Cheating is also a social and motivational problem

Students' choices are shaped by more than rules. Perceived opportunity, peer norms, ambiguous expectations, rationalization, workload or pressure, capability, help-seeking, and expected consequences can interact.

Shame-heavy or surveillance-heavy approaches can also encourage hiding rather than candid disclosure. Clear examples, achievable honest pathways, formative help, visible responsible norms, and predictable fair follow-up address more of the environment.

Knowledge-base synthesis: Academic Integrity. The concept page distinguishes broad synthesis from the individual rationalization, shame/guilt, and cross-cultural studies it summarizes.

Proctoring is a narrow tool, not an integrity strategy

Secure supervision can sometimes be appropriate when the learning claim genuinely requires individual, closed-resource performance. It should not be the automatic response to every AI-capable task.

First ask whether a more reasoning-intensive, process-visible, or transfer-based assessment can measure the same capability. When proctoring is necessary, account for accommodations, privacy, anxiety, technology failure, private-space requirements, and human review.

Related knowledge-base framing: Academic Integrity and Assessment Validity.

Equity is part of validity

An oral defense, webcam recording, timed assessment, personalized project, or synchronous meeting may make outsourcing more difficult, but it can also introduce construct-irrelevant barriers.

The aim is not to make assessment maximally difficult. It is to obtain credible evidence of the intended learning through a modality that is accessible and proportionate.

Evidence caution: not every attractive assessment redesign has direct experimental evidence that it reduces cheating. The strongest rationale for many recommendations is assessment validity, learning science, transparent evidence, reduced opportunity for substitution, and better calibration—not a promise that any one technique makes cheating impossible.

Likewise, evidence that feedback, self-regulation support, belonging, or academic support improves learning or persistence should not be misrepresented as direct proof that those interventions reduce misconduct.

Quick response when a submission raises concern
  1. Re-read the exact assignment-level AI and collaboration rule.
  2. Review evidence already required of all students: sources, drafts, calculations, logs, data, or versions.
  3. Check substantive issues such as nonexistent sources, unexplained method changes, or unsupported reasoning.
  4. Give the student an opportunity to explain consequential decisions or reproduce a small amount of target reasoning under fair conditions.
  5. Treat technical signals, if used at all, as corroborating information rather than proof.
  6. Follow institutional procedures and due-process requirements.
Useful policy boundary questions to answer for students
  • May I ask AI to brainstorm ideas?
  • May I ask it to create an outline?
  • May I ask it to critique a draft?
  • May I have it rewrite a sentence or paragraph?
  • May I paste AI-generated code into my program?
  • May I use AI to analyze my data?
  • May I use AI to find sources, and how must I verify them?
  • Must I disclose the tool, prompts, excerpts, or decisions?
  • What must I still be able to explain or demonstrate independently?
