Redesign for Learning

Redesign assignments for academic integrity in the AI era without turning the course into a surveillance system. Clarify what students are learning, what they should do, what successful work looks like, where AI belongs, and what evidence makes learning visible.

Clarify purpose, tasks, and success criteria. Make learning visible. Clarify AI use. Verify what matters.

A useful redesign pattern

Keep the important learning goal. Improve the clarity of the assignment and change the evidence you collect about how the student learned, decided, checked, revised, and can explain the work.

Independent attempt or prediction
Allowed resources or AI support
Visible revision or reasoning
Brief verification

Design principle: collect enough evidence to make the learning claim defensible, but not so much documentation that students experience every assignment as surveillance.

Step 1

Describe the course and assignment

Course format changes what forms of process evidence and verification are practical. Assignment type changes which redesign strategies are most useful.

A short description is enough. You will build the redesigned student-facing version below.

Step 2

Protect the learning claim

Before deciding whether AI is allowed, identify what the grade is supposed to tell you about the student.

Ask: What should a successful student be able to do because of this assignment—and which parts must students be able to perform without AI doing that thinking for them?

Related research synthesis: Assessment Validity in AI Education .

Which capabilities does this assignment assess?

Prefer observable performances: analyze, calculate, create, defend, diagnose, evaluate, interpret, justify, revise, synthesize, or apply.

Step 3

Make the assignment transparent to students

Build the student-facing prompt around Purpose, Tasks, and Criteria for Success. Put important deadlines where students will see them, make the workflow visible, and provide enough information for students to judge the quality of their own work while they are doing it.

Transparency is not simply “more directions.” Explain why the work matters, what actions students should take, what resources they need, and what successful work looks like. Then ask students to interpret those expectations before they begin.

PURPOSE — why students are doing this work

Connect the work to the learning outcome, course goals, disciplinary practice, and—when appropriate—students' future academic, civic, or professional work.

For introductory students, consider emphasizing only one or two genuinely new skills at a time.

TASKS — what students should do and in what sequence

State what to submit, the sequence of work, important checkpoints, and what resources are permitted. Do not assume students can infer the workflow from the final product alone.

CRITERIA FOR SUCCESS — what good work looks like

An exemplar should not simply be copied. Ask students to identify the characteristics that make it effective and distinguish excellent, adequate, and weak work.

Technical how-to guidance to include for students

Select procedures students may need. The built assignment will include concise directions. Add institution-specific directions below when your LMS or campus tools differ.

A useful policy tells students what is permitted on this task, why the boundary exists, what must remain their own work, and what they should disclose.

Step 5

Choose redesign strategies

Recommended strategies change with the assignment type. Use only the strategies that provide meaningful evidence for your learning goal.

More is not automatically better. A proposal, several logs, multiple recordings, screenshots, complete AI transcripts, an oral defense, and a final reflection may create burden without improving validity. Choose the smallest useful evidence set.

Step 6

Browse redesign examples

Examples change with the assignment type. Use them as patterns rather than requirements.

Step 7

Choose process evidence and verification

Verification should answer a learning question—not attempt to infer authorship from writing style. Keep it short and proportionate.

Useful process evidence

Video is a verification option, not a universal requirement. When speaking on camera is not part of the intended learning, a written, audio, annotated, asynchronous, or live equivalent may provide the same evidence with fewer barriers.

Suggested grading emphasis

Consider grading meaningful process and revision rather than only the polished final product.

Step 8

Run an equity, accessibility, and proportionality check

An assignment can become harder to outsource while also becoming harder for students with disabilities, caregiving obligations, unreliable technology, limited private space, or language barriers. Protect the learning construct—not unnecessary barriers.

Why redesign this way?

Academic integrity in the AI era is not simply a contest between better generation and better detection. The stronger educational question is: what evidence lets us make a defensible claim about what this student knows, can do, and can explain?

Misconception: “Any AI use is cheating.”

AI use is not one behavior. Brainstorming, proofreading, generating a complete answer, critiquing a model, and receiving a hint have very different relationships to the intended learning.

Decide where AI would substitute for the assessed capability, where it can support it, and where responsible AI use is itself part of the competency.

Academic Integrity · Assessment Validity

Misconception: “A finished product proves learning.”

Assisted performance and independent capability are not the same. Generative AI can produce a plausible product while bypassing some of the reasoning, retrieval, drafting, calculation, or revision the assignment was designed to exercise.

A small amount of process evidence plus a brief explanation or transfer task can often make the learning claim more defensible.

Cognitive Offloading

Cognitive surrender and over-reliance

Cognitive offloading is not inherently bad. Notes, calculators, search, and AI can free attention for more complex work.

The educational risk appears when the tool substitutes for the thinking students need to practice or when learners accept AI output without retaining the evaluative role.

A useful pattern is: think first → use AI second → reflect and verify afterward.

Cognitive Offloading

Misconception: “Confident AI is probably right.”

Fluent language is not evidence. Productive AI literacy includes trust calibration: matching confidence to the evidence, task, stakes, and reliability of the system.

A practical rule is: AI proposes; the learner verifies.

Trust Calibration

Misconception: “AI detectors prove authorship.”

Detector output does not directly establish how a submission was produced and can create false-positive, false-negative, fairness, and automation-bias concerns.

Use task rules, process evidence, source checks, substantive inconsistencies, student explanation, and ordinary due process.

Academic Integrity

Cheating is also a social and motivational problem

Choices about academic integrity can be influenced by opportunity, peer norms, ambiguous expectations, rationalization, workload or pressure, help-seeking, and expected consequences.

Clear expectations, realistic honest pathways, formative help, visible responsible norms, and predictable fair follow-up address more of the environment than shame-heavy messaging alone.

Proctoring is a narrow tool, not a complete integrity strategy

Secure supervision can be appropriate when the learning claim truly requires individual, closed-resource performance.

Before adding surveillance, ask whether a more reasoning-intensive, process-visible, interactive, authentic, or transfer-based assessment can measure the same capability with fewer privacy, accessibility, and technology burdens.

Equity is part of validity

An oral defense, webcam recording, timed assessment, or synchronous meeting may make outsourcing harder while also adding barriers that are unrelated to the intended learning.

The aim is not to make assessment maximally difficult. It is to obtain credible evidence of the intended learning through an accessible, proportionate method.

Evidence caution: not every attractive assessment redesign has direct experimental evidence that it reduces cheating. Many recommendations are best justified through assessment validity, transparent expectations, learning science, reduced opportunity for substitution, and better calibration—not a promise that one technique makes cheating impossible.

Quick response when a submission raises concern
  1. Re-read the exact assignment-level AI and collaboration rule.
  2. Review evidence already required of all students: sources, drafts, calculations, logs, data, or versions.
  3. Check substantive issues such as nonexistent sources, unexplained method changes, or unsupported reasoning.
  4. Give the student an opportunity to explain consequential decisions or reproduce a small amount of target reasoning under fair conditions.
  5. Treat technical signals, if used at all, as corroborating information rather than proof.
  6. Follow institutional procedures and due-process requirements.
Useful AI-policy boundary questions to answer for students
  • May I ask AI to brainstorm ideas?
  • May I ask it to create an outline?
  • May I ask it to critique a draft?
  • May I have it rewrite a sentence or paragraph?
  • May I paste AI-generated code into my program?
  • May I use AI to analyze my data?
  • May I use AI to find sources?
  • How must AI-suggested sources be verified?
  • What must I disclose?
  • What must I still be able to explain or demonstrate independently?
